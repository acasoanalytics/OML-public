Endogenous VOCs: good enough for Plasmodium, not the FDA

An endogenous VOC can be real, evolved and reliably sensed, and still make a poor test. Selection acts across millions of bites, so a bias of a few percent is enough. A diagnosis acts on one person, where the overlap between infected and uninfected sets the limit. Set the separation and the noise, then compare the two.

Conclusion

At AUC 0.63 a test is wrong in more than one pairing in three. Selection is not held to that standard, because it acts in aggregate rather than on any single bite. A marker moving the odds from 500 in 1,000 to 630 is useless on an individual and a large fitness gain to a parasite transmitted hundreds of millions of times a year. Plasmodium appears to have found exactly this: infected people release more heptanal, octanal and nonanal, mosquito antennae respond to them, and adding them to a healthy odour blend raises attraction (Robinson et al., PNAS 2018; Lacroix et al., PLoS Biology 2005).

An evolved signal is therefore weak evidence that a good test can be built from it. These defaults are set to a published asymptomatic malaria breath panel: AUC 0.63, sensitivity 47%, specificity 71%. Mosquitoes bite the infected host 63 times in 100. Screening with it at 2% prevalence finds 9 true cases per 1,000 tested and flags 285 people who are not infected.

Equal-variance normal model. Noise adds in quadrature, so measured d = true d / √(1 + noise²) and AUC = Φ(d/√2). Youden's J is sensitivity + specificity − 1, maximised at a cut-off of d/2 where J = 2Φ(d/2) − 1. The mosquito is a noiseless comparator, choosing the stronger of the two sampled emissions, so its expected hit rate is the AUC. Confidence interval is Wilson, 95%.
